Title: System Thinking
Date: 2026-03-05 16:04
Author: Alec Munro
Tags: misc
Slug: system-thinking


I wanted to write about System Thinking, as it applies to me.

I am able to evaluate conceptual systems in my head, running simulations through them to try to solve problems. I've known for a long time that most people can't/don't do that, and that it might be a sign I'm on the autism spectrum. 

About a decade ago I discovered I have aphantasia, which in my case means I have no visual memory, though I do see things in my head, they are impossible to pin down and extract information from. I've come to suspect that this relates to my way of thinking, because I'm unable to process the world using images, and rely on managing concepts instead. I haven't done any research on the connection between aphantasia and systems thinking, but I do understand there to be a connection between aphantasia and autism.

Systems thinking, as I've long understood it, is considered a way to approach problems. A technique that can be taught and replicated at scale, though certain folk seem to pick it up easier.

But over the last decade, it's become increasingly clear that I do something almost nobody does, in the way I deal with problems, my work, and life in general, which I've taken to calling System Thinking. System Thinking, singular, because in my brain, there aren't separate systems being evaluated independently, but only a single system that encompasses everything, with connections of various strength getting tested and refactored for efficiency.

To explain this, let's consider the classic "best ideas in the shower", along with my own sales pitch over the years to potential employers. As to the first, it is well-known that programmers have their best ideas in the shower, or doing something else mechanical and monotonous that allows their brain to approach existing problems from a new perspective. As I understand, this is also generally applicable in most Systems Thinking work, that requires maintaining a lot of state in your head. I've been a big believer in this concept, though I like washing the dishes in particular as a way to let my mind change gears.

I've never really had much of a social life or hobbies, and found my employment would always creep into my thoughts, regardless of any attempt I made to establish work/life boundaries. So I gave up on that a while back, and I tell employers that because I'll be thinking about their problems all the time, I'll contribute well outside of my established areas of responsibility. While this is true, in terms of the breadth of my contributions, I've realized it's not the whole story.

I don't think about work problems all the time because I have nothing else to do. I've tried to fill my time with gaming and other distractions, but even those get blended seamlessly into my singular mental landscape.

I think about work problems all the time, because I think about EVERYTHING all the time. When I'm taking out the trash, and I see a magazine headline about the best schools in Cincinnati, my brain will dig up what it knows about who is selling our software in Ohio, and earmark if we can use the article.

To give one more example of how this manifests: I've been working remotely for 15+ years. While I've developed plenty of specific techniques to make this efficient, I've also noticed I have something of a sixth sense in terms of reading organizational subtext in electronic communication. At this point, my theory is that because everything in my brain is in one giant simulation, that is being used for predicting the future and recommending valuable actions, I notice when my predictions start to be wrong, because my understanding has diverged from the underlying reality.

In the last couple of years, I haven't been drinking, and I've been working to build strong work-peer relationships. This has enabled me to observe that most people, even those with a fair bit more time in leadership roles, don't have this sixth sense, and are often surprised by organizational changes. For me, if I think about where I want our product to be in 5 years, I'm also going to think about how our organizational will need to be shaped to deliver that product, and start mapping out the necessary transitions to get there from here.

I know that's just a planning activity many engineers will be familiar with, but it's happening constantly in my head, and in there, I'm also considering what my kids college tuition will be at that point, who might be President, and whether my old colleague from 20 years ago would be interested in working with me on some aspect of it. Then, when I start talking post-secondary education with Ruby, I'm also doing it in the context of this System.
